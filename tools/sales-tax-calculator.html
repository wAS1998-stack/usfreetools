What This Calculator Does

This calculator handles everyday sales tax math in both directions. Enter a price and your local tax rate to see the tax amount and the final total you will pay at the register. Or flip it: enter a receipt total and the rate, and it backs out the pre-tax price. Both are instant and exact to the cent.

Because US sales tax varies by state, county, and city, there is no single national rate to memorize — you enter yours, and the calculator does the rest. It is the quick answer to “what will this actually cost?” and the reverse question expense reports keep asking: “what was the price before tax?”

How US Sales Tax Works

Sales tax in the United States is layered. States set a base rate — from zero in a handful of states like Oregon, Delaware, Montana, and New Hampshire, up past 7% elsewhere — and counties, cities, and special districts can add their own percentages on top. The combined rate is what you actually pay, and it can exceed 10% in some localities. This is why the same item costs different amounts in different ZIP codes, and why the calculator asks for your local combined rate rather than assuming one.

The math itself is simple multiplication: tax equals price times the rate, and the total is price plus tax. The reverse calculation is the one people get wrong — to remove tax from a total you divide by (1 + rate), not subtract the percentage, because the tax was computed on the pre-tax price, not the total. A $108.25 total at 8.25% was a $100 item; subtracting 8.25% of the total would give the wrong answer. The calculator applies the correct formula in both directions.

Where This Calculation Helps

Shopping is the everyday case: knowing the true out-the-door price before you reach the register, comparing an online price (often untaxed at listing) against a local one, or budgeting a large purchase where the tax alone is significant — 8% on a $1,200 laptop is nearly $100. For anyone managing spending closely, calculating totals in advance keeps the checkout number from being a surprise.

The reverse mode earns its keep in paperwork. Expense reports frequently need pre-tax amounts; invoices and budgets separate the item cost from the tax; resellers and small businesses back tax out of gross receipts. Dividing by (1 + rate) by hand invites errors, and the calculator makes it a two-second task. One honest note: this tool computes with whatever rate you give it — it does not know your jurisdiction's rate or what items are exempt (groceries and clothing are untaxed or reduced in some states). Your state or local revenue website lists the exact rate; this calculator handles the math.

Sales Tax Across the States: The Practical Landscape

The US map of sales tax is more varied than most shoppers assume. Five states charge no state sales tax at all — Alaska, Delaware, Montana, New Hampshire, and Oregon (though some Alaskan localities levy their own). At the other end, combined state-plus-local rates climb past 9–10% in parts of states like Louisiana, Tennessee, Arkansas, Washington, and Alabama. California carries the highest state base rate at 7.25% before local additions. Between those poles, most Americans pay a combined rate somewhere in the 6–9% range — and because the local component varies by city and county, two stores a few miles apart can charge measurably different totals on the same item.

What gets taxed varies as much as the rate. Many states exempt or reduce tax on groceries; others tax them fully. Clothing is exempt in a handful of states like Minnesota, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania (with thresholds and exceptions), and prescription medicines are untaxed almost everywhere. Online shopping changed too: since the 2018 Wayfair decision, large online retailers generally collect sales tax based on your delivery address, closing the old “tax-free internet” era. Several states also hold annual tax-free holidays for back-to-school or emergency supplies. None of this changes the arithmetic this calculator performs — but knowing your own combined rate, and which of your purchases it actually applies to, is what turns the math into accurate budgeting.

Quick Tips

  • Tax = price × rate; total = price × (1 + rate).
  • To remove tax from a total, divide by (1 + rate) — do not subtract the percentage.
  • US rates combine state + local and can exceed 10%; check your exact local rate.
  • Some states tax groceries and clothing differently or not at all.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate sales tax?

Multiply the price by the tax rate as a decimal. A $50 item at 8.25% tax is 50 x 0.0825 = $4.13 tax, for a $54.13 total. Enter your price and local rate and the calculator does it instantly.

Why does the sales tax rate vary so much in the US?

Because it is set by states, counties, and cities combined. State rates range from 0% (like Oregon and Delaware) to over 7%, and local additions can push combined rates past 10% in some areas — which is why you enter your local rate rather than one national figure.

How do I find the pre-tax price from a total?

Divide the total by (1 + rate). A $54.13 receipt in an 8.25% area: 54.13 / 1.0825 = $50.00 before tax. The calculator's reverse mode does this — useful for expense reports and price comparisons.

Is this tax advice?

No — it is arithmetic on the rate you enter. What is taxable, exemptions, and exact local rates vary by jurisdiction; check your state or local revenue website for your precise rate, and a tax professional for anything beyond simple purchase math.

Which states have no sales tax?

Delaware, Montana, New Hampshire and Oregon have no general sales tax. Alaska has no statewide sales tax, but many Alaskan cities and boroughs add their own, so purchases there can still be taxed.

Which state has the highest sales tax?

California has the highest statewide rate at 7.25%. Once local taxes are included, Louisiana has the highest average combined rate at about 10.13%, followed by Tennessee (9.61%) and Washington (9.57%), per the Tax Foundation’s July 2026 figures.

How much is the tax on $500?

Multiply $500 by your rate as a decimal: at 6% the tax is $30 (total $530), at 7% it is $35 ($535), at 8.25% it is $41.25 ($541.25) and at 10% it is $50 ($550).
