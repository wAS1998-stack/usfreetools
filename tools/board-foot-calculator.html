What This Calculator Does

This calculator computes board feet — the volume unit hardwood lumber is sold by. Enter a board's thickness and width in inches, its length in feet, and how many pieces, and it totals the board footage; add a price per board foot and it totals the cost. It is the arithmetic every hardwood purchase and project estimate runs on.

Board feet confuse newcomers because the unit mixes inches and feet and the pricing differs from the per-piece world of construction lumber. The formula is genuinely simple once seen — and having it computed live turns lumberyard browsing and project budgeting into quick, confident math.

The Board Foot, Explained Properly

One board foot is 144 cubic inches of wood — the volume of a piece 1″ thick × 12″ wide × 12″ long. The working formula: thickness (inches) × width (inches) × length (feet) ÷ 12. A 1 × 6 that is 8 feet long: 1 × 6 × 8 ÷ 12 = 4 board feet. Double the thickness and the footage doubles — an 8/4 (2-inch) board of the same face is 8 bf — which is precisely why thick stock costs so much more than its face area suggests.

Hardwood dealers describe thickness in quarters: 4/4 (“four-quarter”) is 1 inch, 5/4 is 1¼, 6/4 is 1½, 8/4 is 2 — rough-sawn thickness before surfacing, so a surfaced 4/4 board actually measures around 13/16″ yet is sold as 4/4. Board-foot pricing uses the rough dimensions. This vocabulary plus the formula is the entire literacy needed to read a hardwood price list: cherry at $8.50/bf means the 4-bf board above costs $34, and a project's cut list totals into a budget in minutes.

Nominal Sizes and the Two Lumber Worlds

US lumber lives in two pricing worlds, and mixing them causes most beginner errors. Construction softwood — the 2×4s and 2×6s of framing — is sold per piece in nominal sizes that overstate reality: a “2×4” measures 1.5 × 3.5 inches after drying and surfacing, a “1×6” is 0.75 × 5.5. Nobody computes board feet at the home center; you buy sticks. Hardwood — oak, maple, walnut, cherry from a lumberyard — is sold by the board foot at rough quarter-sawn thicknesses, and there the formula governs every transaction.

The practical rule: use actual (or quarter-system) dimensions for board-foot math, never nominal names. Computing a “2×4” as 2 × 4 inches overstates its wood by 52% — harmless when buying per piece, badly misleading when estimating material for a project or comparing hardwood against softwood costs. When a hardwood dealer lists widths, those are measured widths (often random widths per board, tallied at the desk); your cut-list estimate simply needs to total at least the project's footage plus waste, since the yard's tally of the actual boards is what you pay.

Estimating a Project Without Surprises

Project estimating is cut-list math plus honesty about waste. Total the board feet of every finished part, then add 20–30% for hardwood: defects to cut around, grain matching, jointing and planing losses (that 4/4 board finishing at 13/16″ already ate an eighth), test cuts, and the offcuts too short to use. Fine furniture makers with picky grain standards run higher; painted shop projects run lower. The waste percentage is not padding — footage bought equals footage of tree, not footage of furniture.

Two buying habits complete the skill. First, price-compare across thicknesses correctly: 8/4 stock costs more per board foot than 4/4 (thicker boards are scarcer), so resawing thick stock is not automatically economical — run both numbers. Second, when a project needs specific widths, remember hardwood comes in random widths: buying 40 bf does not guarantee four 6-inch-wide boards, so shop the actual stack or order specified widths at a premium. With the calculator totaling footage and cost as the cut list evolves, the lumberyard visit becomes selection rather than arithmetic — which is exactly where the attention belongs.

Quick Tips

  • Board feet = thickness″ × width″ × length′ ÷ 12, times quantity.
  • 4/4 = 1″ rough, 8/4 = 2″ — hardwood thickness talks in quarters.
  • Use actual dimensions, never nominal (“2×4” is really 1.5 × 3.5).
  • Add 20-30% waste for hardwood projects; footage bought ≠ footage of furniture.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a board foot?

The lumber volume unit: 1 board foot is 144 cubic inches — a piece 1 inch thick by 12 inches wide by 1 foot long. Hardwood is priced by the board foot, so buying it means computing it.

How do I calculate board feet?

Thickness (in) x width (in) x length (ft) ÷ 12, times quantity. A 1 x 6 board 8 feet long is 4 board feet; ten of them are 40. The calculator totals and prices the stack.

Do I use nominal or actual dimensions?

For hardwood priced by board foot, use the actual measured dimensions (or the quarter-system thickness like 4/4, 8/4). Softwood construction lumber's nominal sizes (a '2x4' is really 1.5 x 3.5) are priced per piece, not by board foot.

What does 4/4 or 8/4 lumber mean?

Hardwood thickness in quarters of an inch: 4/4 is 1 inch, 5/4 is 1.25, 8/4 is 2 inches — rough thickness before surfacing. Board-foot math uses these quarter thicknesses, which is why an 8/4 board costs twice the footage of a 4/4 of the same face size.
