How Coffee Ratios Work

A brew ratio expresses grams of coffee per grams of water — and since a milliliter of water weighs a gram, 1:16 simply means 1 g of coffee per 16 ml of water. For a 240 ml cup, that is 15 g of coffee; for a 1-liter brew, 62.5 g. The widely cited sweet spot for filter methods sits between 1:15 and 1:17: lower ratios (1:15) brew stronger and more intense, higher ones (1:17) lighter and more delicate, with 1:16 a balanced, popular default.

The reason ratios beat scoops is precision. A “scoop” of coffee varies with grind size, bean density, and how heaped it is — the same spoon can hold noticeably different weights, which is why scoop-measured coffee tastes different day to day. Weighing coffee in grams against a measured water amount removes that variation entirely. It also makes scaling trivial: double the water, double the coffee, and the cup tastes the same, which no scoop-counting reliably achieves.

Dialing In Your Perfect Cup

The practical way to use ratios is as a starting point you adjust to taste. Begin at 1:16. If the cup tastes weak or watery, step to 1:15 (more coffee per water); if it is heavy or bitter for your taste, try 1:17. Change only the ratio while keeping everything else the same, and you will quickly find your personal preference — most people settle within a step or two. Once found, the ratio makes that exact cup repeatable forever, for any batch size.

Ratios interact with brew method too. Pour over and drip sit happily across the whole 1:15–1:17 range. French press drinkers often lean slightly stronger, toward 1:14–1:15, since the metal filter's fuller body suits it. Cold brew concentrate uses much stronger ratios (around 1:5 to 1:8) and is then diluted. Espresso is its own world at roughly 1:2. Whatever your method, the workflow is the same: pick the ratio, weigh the coffee this calculator gives you, and brew — the consistency alone will improve your coffee more than any gadget.

Beyond the Ratio: Grind, Water, and Time

The ratio sets strength, but three companion variables decide whether that strength tastes good. Grind size controls how fast water extracts flavor: finer grinds extract faster and suit short brews (espresso, moka), coarser grinds suit long immersion (French press, cold brew), with pour-over in the middle. If your correctly-ratioed cup tastes sour and weak, the grind is likely too coarse (under-extraction); harsh and bitter suggests too fine (over-extraction). Adjust grind one notch at a time while holding the ratio constant — changing both at once makes it impossible to know what helped.

Water temperature matters more than most beginners expect: the commonly cited sweet spot is roughly 90–96°C (195–205°F). Water straight off a rolling boil, rested thirty seconds, lands in range. Cooler water under-extracts toward sourness. Time is the third lever — around 2.5–3.5 minutes for pour-over, 4 for French press — and it interacts with grind: coarser needs longer. The practical method that actually improves coffee is single-variable iteration: lock the ratio with this calculator, keep water and time consistent, and tune grind to taste. Once the cup is where you want it, the numbers make it repeatable every morning — which is the entire point of measuring.

Quick Tips

  • Start at 1:16 — about 15 g of coffee for a 240 ml cup — and adjust one step to taste.
  • Weigh coffee in grams; scoops vary too much to be consistent.
  • Stronger cup: move toward 1:15. Lighter cup: move toward 1:17.
  • Scaling is easy — double the water, double the coffee, identical strength.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best coffee-to-water ratio?

Most specialty coffee guidance lands between 1:15 and 1:17 — one gram of coffee per 15 to 17 grams (milliliters) of water. 1:16 is a widely used starting point; stronger tastes go toward 1:15, lighter toward 1:17.

How much coffee do I need for one cup?

For a 240 ml cup at a 1:16 ratio, you need about 15 grams of coffee. The calculator does this instantly for any water amount and ratio, so you can scale to any number of cups.

Does the ratio change for French press or pour over?

The same 1:15–1:17 range works across pour over, drip, and French press; some prefer slightly stronger (closer to 1:14–1:15) for French press. Espresso is different territory entirely, using far stronger ratios around 1:2.

Should I measure coffee by weight or by spoons?

By weight, ideally. Grams are consistent, while spoon volume varies hugely with grind size and bean density — the same scoop can hold quite different amounts of coffee. A cheap kitchen scale makes brewing dramatically more repeatable.
