🌿 Grass Seed Calculator

Enter your lawn’s area and choose new seeding or overseeding to see the pounds of seed to buy — using standard rates per 1,000 square feet.

What This Calculator Does

This calculator turns a lawn project into a seed purchase. Enter the area in square feet, choose whether you are seeding bare ground or overseeding existing turf, and pick the rate for your grass type — it returns the pounds of seed to buy. The per-1,000-square-foot rates printed on seed bags become an exact number for your lawn.

Seed is one of the few materials where both directions of error hurt the result itself: too little produces a patchy stand that weeds colonize, and too much crowds seedlings into weak, thin turf. The right rate is not conservative or generous — it is agronomic, and the calculator applies it.

Rates, Species, and Why They Differ So Much

Seeding rates are quoted per 1,000 sq ft and vary enormously by species because seed sizes do. Tall fescue — the workhorse of transition-zone US lawns — runs about 8 lbs per 1,000 for new seeding; perennial ryegrass similar at 8-9. Kentucky bluegrass needs only 2-3 lbs, not because it is sparse but because its seeds are tiny (over a million per pound) and it spreads by rhizomes once established. Warm-season Bermuda seeds at just 1-2 lbs. The formula is uniform: area ÷ 1,000 × rate — 5,000 sq ft of new fescue is 5 × 8 = 40 lbs.

Overseeding — sowing into existing turf to thicken it — halves the rate (fescue ≈ 4 lbs/1,000) because established grass already holds most of the ground and the new seedlings fill gaps rather than build a stand from nothing. Seed blends print their own rate on the bag reflecting the mix; when in doubt, the bag's number wins over any generic table, and this calculator's custom-rate option accepts it directly. Measure the actual lawn (decompose odd shapes into rectangles, subtract beds and hardscape) rather than guessing — lawns are chronically overestimated by eye.

Why Over-Seeding Backfires

The instinct to “throw extra down to be sure” is the most common seeding mistake, and the failure mode is biological. Each seedling needs light, water, and soil nutrients; doubled density means every plant gets half, producing spindly, shallow-rooted grass that looks lush at two weeks and collapses by mid-season — thin blades, damping-off disease in the crowded canopy, and poor drought tolerance from underdeveloped roots. Lawns seeded at rate look sparser early and dramatically better by month three, which is exactly backward from what impatient eyes expect.

Under-seeding fails more visibly but no less surely: coverage gaps become weed real estate, since bare soil in a lawn is an invitation crabgrass reliably accepts, and patch-fixing later costs more seed and effort than correct density up front. The rate on the bag is decades of turf science compressed to one number — trust it, distribute it evenly (a broadcast spreader in two perpendicular passes at half-rate each beats one heavy pass), and give germination its two honest weeks of consistent moisture. Even distribution at the right rate is most of what separates lawns that establish from lawns that get reseeded.

Timing and the First Six Weeks

When you seed matters nearly as much as how much. Cool-season grasses (fescue, bluegrass, rye — most of the northern and transition-zone US) establish best in early fall: soil still warm for fast germination, air cooling, annual weeds finished for the year, and a full mild season to root before summer stress. Spring is the workable second choice, accepting more weed competition. Warm-season species (Bermuda, zoysia) invert the calendar, seeding in late spring as soil passes about 65°F. Fighting the calendar wastes seed the math got right.

The first six weeks decide the outcome: keep the top half-inch of soil consistently moist (light watering once or twice daily until germination, then deeper and less frequent as roots chase the moisture down), hold off mowing until the new grass reaches about 3 inches, and skip weed treatments until the lawn has been mowed a few times — most herbicides read seedlings as weeds. Starter fertilizer at seeding, per its own bag rate, supports establishment. None of this is exotic; it is simply that seed, rate, timing, and water are a system, and the calculator supplies the one input people most often guess.

Quick Tips

  • Pounds = (area ÷ 1,000) × rate; fescue ≈ 8 new / 4 overseed per 1,000 sq ft.
  • More seed is worse, not safer — crowded seedlings grow weak turf.
  • Cool-season grasses: seed in early fall. Warm-season: late spring.
  • Spread half-rate in two perpendicular passes for even coverage.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much grass seed do I need per square foot?

Rates run per 1,000 sq ft: tall fescue about 8 lbs for a new lawn and 4 for overseeding; Kentucky bluegrass 2-3 new (its tiny seeds go further); ryegrass 8-9; Bermuda 1-2. Multiply your area ÷ 1,000 by the rate — 5,000 sq ft of new fescue needs about 40 lbs.

Is more seed better?

No — overseeding past the rate crowds seedlings into competing for light and water, producing weak, disease-prone turf. The listed rates are the agronomic sweet spot; heavier is genuinely worse, not safer.

What is the difference between new seeding and overseeding rates?

New lawns seed bare soil and need full coverage; overseeding thickens existing turf where established grass already occupies most of the ground, so roughly half the rate suffices.

When should I plant grass seed?

Cool-season grasses (fescue, bluegrass, rye) establish best in early fall — warm soil, cooling air, fewer weeds — with spring second-best. Warm-season grasses (Bermuda, zoysia) seed in late spring through early summer as soil warms.
