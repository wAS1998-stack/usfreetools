🧰 Drywall Calculator

Enter the room’s dimensions and openings, pick a sheet size, and see how many drywall sheets the walls and ceiling need — with the mud and screws to match.

What This Calculator Does

This calculator counts drywall. Enter the room's length, width, and wall height, the doors and windows, whether the ceiling is included, and the sheet size you plan to hang — it returns the sheet count, with the walls and ceiling computed the way estimators do it. The guidance alongside covers the consumables that travel with every sheet: joint compound, tape, and screws.

Drywall is cheap per sheet and miserable to re-shop mid-job — sheets do not fit in cars, and a stopped job with open walls stays stopped. Counting correctly once, with the standard margin, is the entire game.

The Sheet Math for Walls and Ceilings

Wall surface is perimeter times height: a 12 × 12 room has 48 linear feet of wall, at 8 ft that is 384 sq ft, minus about 21 sq ft per door and 15 per window — call it 348 with one of each. The ceiling adds length × width (144 sq ft), for 492 sq ft total. Divide by the sheet: a 4×8 covers 32 sq ft, so 492 ÷ 32 = 15.4 → 16 sheets. A 4×12 covers 48 sq ft and drops the count to 11 — with meaningfully fewer seams to finish.

Round up and then add the honest margin: 5-10% for simple rooms, more for rooms with many openings, angles, or soffits, because drywall consumes itself in cuts — the piece over a window rarely comes from an offcut that fits. Estimators think in sheets, not square feet, precisely because a sheet that is 80% used is 100% purchased. Closets, stairwell walls, and the garage side of shared walls are the surfaces most often forgotten at count time; a room sketch with every surface labeled beats mental arithmetic.

Choosing Sheets: Size and Thickness

Sheet size trades handling against finishing. Every seam between sheets must be taped, mudded in three coats, and sanded — the slow, skilled part of drywall — so the professional instinct is the biggest sheet the space allows: 4×12s hung horizontally put one continuous seam at a comfortable working height and eliminate the vertical joints 4×8s create every four feet. The DIY counterweight is real: a 1/2″ 4×12 weighs over 80 lbs, needs two sets of hands and clear access, and will not turn a hallway corner that a 4×8 manages.

Thickness follows the surface. Walls standardly take 1/2″; ceilings do better with 5/8″ (stiffer between joists, resisting sag — and required as “Type X” fire-rated board in garages and attached-wall assemblies under most codes; check yours). Moisture-prone rooms use moisture-resistant board, and true wet areas behind tile want cement board rather than any drywall. Lightweight 1/2″ panels split the weight difference for solo hangers. The calculator counts whichever sheet you select; choosing the right one is this paragraph's job.

Mud, Tape, Screws — the Rest of the Order

The consumables scale with the sheet count, and the rules of thumb hold up well. Joint compound: about 0.05 gallons per square foot of board for a full three-coat finish — a 4.5-gallon bucket per 10-12 sheets; buy all-purpose for taping and a lighter topping or all-purpose for finish coats. Tape: roughly one 500-ft roll per 20-ish sheets, paper for flats and either paper or mesh by preference (paper folds crisply into corners; metal-edged or composite bead handles outside corners). Screws: about 32 per 4×8 at 12-16″ spacing — 1-1/4″ coarse-thread for wood studs, fine-thread for steel.

Sequence the hang like the pros: ceilings first (walls then support the ceiling edges), then walls top sheet first, hung horizontally, staggering vertical joints between rows and avoiding seams at door and window corners where cracks start. Screws set just below flush without breaking paper — a drywall screw-setter bit makes this automatic. None of it is exotic skill, but all of it multiplies across sixteen sheets, which is why the count from this calculator, plus its margin, plus one trip for consumables, is the whole shopping strategy.

Quick Tips

  • Surface = perimeter × height − openings, + ceiling; ÷ 32 (4×8) or 48 (4×12).
  • Bigger sheets = fewer seams to finish; smaller sheets = manageable weight.
  • Ceilings: 5/8″ board; garages: fire-rated Type X (check local code).
  • Per 4×8 sheet: ~32 screws; a 4.5-gal mud bucket serves 10-12 sheets.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many sheets of drywall do I need?

Compute the surface: wall perimeter x height minus openings, plus the ceiling (length x width) if you are doing it. Divide by the sheet area — 32 sq ft for 4x8, 48 for 4x12 — and round up. A 12 x 12 room with 8-ft walls, a door, and a window needs about 16 sheets of 4x8 including the ceiling.

Should I use 4x8 or 4x12 sheets?

Bigger sheets mean fewer seams to tape and finish — the labor-intensive part — so pros favor 4x12 where access allows. 4x8 sheets are lighter and turn tighter corners, making them the DIY default. Ceilings often use 5/8-inch sheets; walls commonly 1/2-inch.

How much joint compound and tape per sheet?

Rules of thumb: roughly 0.05 gallons of ready-mix compound per square foot finished (about a 4.5-gallon bucket per 10-12 sheets), tape at about 3 linear feet per sheet-edge seam, and about 32 screws per 4x8 sheet at standard spacing.

Do I subtract doors and windows from drywall?

Yes for the count, with judgment: openings save whole sheets only when large. Standard practice subtracts ~21 sq ft per door and ~15 per window but still rounds sheets up generously, since sheets are cut around openings and offcuts often go to waste.
