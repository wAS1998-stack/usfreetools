What This Calculator Does

This calculator tells you a screen's pixel density — its PPI (pixels per inch) — from two things you can find on any spec sheet: the resolution and the diagonal size. It also shows the total pixel count and the aspect ratio. Enter the numbers for any monitor, laptop, phone, or TV and the figures appear instantly.

Resolution alone is misleading: 1920×1080 is sharp on a 13-inch laptop and visibly coarse on a 27-inch monitor, because the same pixels are stretched over more inches. PPI is the number that captures this — actual sharpness — which makes it the right basis for comparing displays and choosing what to buy.

How Pixel Density Works

PPI comes from straightforward geometry. The screen's diagonal in pixels is the square root of the horizontal pixels squared plus the vertical pixels squared (Pythagoras); dividing that by the physical diagonal in inches gives pixels per inch. A 27-inch 2560×1440 display has a diagonal of about 2938 pixels, so 2938 ÷ 27 ≈ 109 PPI. A 6.1-inch phone at 2532×1170 lands around 457 PPI — which is why phone screens look so much crisper than monitors.

The comparison only makes sense together with viewing distance. Eyes resolve angular detail, so a phone held 30 cm away needs far more PPI than a TV three meters away to look equally sharp — a 55-inch 4K TV is only about 80 PPI yet appears perfectly crisp from the couch. This is the honest answer to “how much PPI do I need”: enough for your distance, and past that point additional density is imperceptible.

Using PPI to Choose and Configure Displays

For desk monitors, PPI predicts your experience better than resolution. Around 90-110 PPI, text renders comfortably at 100% scaling — the classic 24-inch 1080p (~92 PPI) and 27-inch 1440p (~109 PPI) sweet spots. Jumping to a 27-inch 4K (~163 PPI) gives noticeably crisper text but usually requires interface scaling (125-150%) to keep things readable, which is worth knowing before buying. Comparing two candidate monitors by PPI instantly reveals which will actually look sharper on your desk.

PPI matters in other practical corners too. Designers and photographers use it to reason about how on-screen sizes relate to print (where DPI governs output). Developers target density buckets when sizing interface elements and assets for phones versus desktops. And anyone mixing displays — a laptop next to an external monitor — feels PPI mismatches directly, as windows change apparent size when dragged between screens. Knowing each display's PPI explains the behavior and guides scaling settings. Whatever the case, the calculation is the same, and this tool does it instantly from the two numbers on the box.

PPI, DPI, and Display Scaling Untangled

Two adjacent terms cause most of the confusion in this area. PPI is a property of screens — physical pixels per inch of display, which this calculator computes. DPI (dots per inch) properly belongs to printing — ink dots on paper, with 300 DPI the standard for sharp print. The habit of using them interchangeably causes real mistakes, like judging whether an image “is 300 DPI” in the abstract: an image has only pixels; DPI emerges when you choose a print size. A 3000×2400 photo prints at 300 DPI as a 10×8-inch print, or 150 DPI at 20×16. The calculator's PPI figure describes your screen; print math is the same arithmetic pointed at paper.

Display scaling is the third piece. On high-PPI screens, rendering interface elements at their native pixel size would make them microscopically small, so operating systems scale the UI — 150% or 200% — drawing everything with more pixels at the same physical size. This is why a 27-inch 4K monitor at 150% scaling shows the workspace of a 1440p display but noticeably sharper: the extra density goes into crispness, not more space. The practical takeaway when choosing monitors: decide the physical size and workspace you want first, then let PPI tell you how sharp it will be and what scaling you will run. Density is quality; scaling is how modern systems spend it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is PPI?

Pixels per inch — how densely a screen packs its pixels. It is calculated from the resolution and the physical diagonal: the diagonal pixel count divided by the diagonal inches. Higher PPI means finer detail and sharper text at the same viewing distance.

How is PPI calculated?

Take the square root of (horizontal pixels squared plus vertical pixels squared) — the diagonal in pixels — and divide by the diagonal size in inches. A 27-inch 2560x1440 monitor works out to about 109 PPI.

What is a good PPI for a monitor?

Roughly 90-110 PPI is comfortable for desktop monitors at arm's length; 140-220+ gives the 'retina'-class sharpness of high-DPI displays. Phones, viewed much closer, commonly run 300-500+ PPI. Viewing distance is what decides how much PPI you can actually perceive.

Does higher PPI always look better?

Only up to what your eyes can resolve at your viewing distance — beyond that, extra density is invisible. Higher PPI also means more pixels for the graphics hardware to drive, so the practical sweet spot balances sharpness, performance, and cost.

What is the PPI of a 27-inch 1440p monitor?

About 109 PPI. The same panel size at 4K is about 163 PPI and at 1080p only about 82 PPI, which is why 1080p looks soft on a 27-inch screen at desk distance.

What is dot pitch?

Dot pitch (or pixel pitch) is the distance between the centers of neighboring pixels, usually in millimeters. It is the inverse of PPI: dot pitch = 25.4 ÷ PPI, so a 109 PPI monitor has a 0.233 mm pitch. Smaller pitch means a sharper image.

What PPI counts as a “retina” display?

It depends on how far away you sit. At about 12 inches (a phone) you need roughly 300 PPI before pixels disappear; at 24 inches (a desktop monitor) about 140 PPI is enough; across a living room a 4K TV is already past the limit.
