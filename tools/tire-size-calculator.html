🛞 Tire Size Calculator

Enter two tire sizes in the standard 225/45R17 format and compare their true diameters, circumference, and the speedometer error a change would cause.

What This Calculator Does

This calculator decodes and compares tire sizes. Enter two sizes in the standard sidewall format — width, aspect ratio, and wheel diameter, like 225/45R17 — and it computes each tire's true overall diameter and circumference, then shows the difference between them and the speedometer error the change would cause. It turns the cryptic sidewall code into numbers you can actually reason about.

Whether you are considering plus-sizing wheels, choosing winter tires, or checking whether a bargain set will behave like your originals, the question underneath is always the same: how big is this tire really, and how different is it from what the car expects? That is exactly what this tool answers.

Reading the Sidewall Code

The standard code packs three measurements in mixed units. The first number (225) is the tread width in millimeters. The second (45) is the aspect ratio — the sidewall's height expressed as a percentage of the width, so a 45-series tire on a 225 width has sidewalls of about 101 mm. The letter R denotes radial construction (nearly universal today), and the final number (17) is the wheel diameter it fits, in inches. Additional markings — load index and speed rating — follow the size and matter for safety, though they do not change the geometry this calculator computes.

Overall diameter is the wheel plus two sidewalls: wheel inches times 25.4, plus twice (width times aspect ratio). For 225/45R17 that is 431.8 + 202.5 = about 634 mm, roughly 25 inches. Circumference — the distance covered each rotation — is that diameter times pi. These two derived figures are what actually matter on the road, which is why two sizes with very different-looking codes (say 225/45R17 and 205/50R17) can be nearly identical in practice, while an innocent-looking change in one number shifts the geometry meaningfully.

Why Diameter Differences Matter

The speedometer and odometer count wheel rotations calibrated to the factory circumference. Fit a tire 3% larger and every rotation covers 3% more road than the car assumes: the speedometer reads 60 when you are truly doing about 61.8, and the odometer under-counts your miles. Smaller tires do the opposite — you travel slower than indicated and the odometer over-counts. The calculator's error percentage tells you exactly how a candidate size shifts this, which matters for speed enforcement, lease mileage, and fuel-economy math alike.

Diameter also touches more than instruments. Effective gearing changes — larger tires slightly lengthen it, dulling acceleration and lowering cruising RPM — and clearance to fenders and suspension shrinks. Modern safety systems (ABS, stability control) are calibrated around expected wheel speeds, and mismatched diameters side-to-side or beyond tolerance can degrade them. This is the basis of the widely used 3% guideline: within it, a size swap is generally transparent; beyond it, the change deserves professional confirmation. The calculator makes the 3% check a five-second task before any purchase.

Plus-Sizing and Common Swaps

The classic reason to compare sizes is plus-sizing: moving to a larger wheel while keeping the overall diameter constant by reducing the sidewall. Going from 225/45R17 toward an 18-inch wheel, the aspect ratio drops (to a 40-series, for example) so the total stays near the original — the calculator confirms whether a proposed combination truly lands within tolerance. The trade is aesthetic and handling sharpness against ride comfort and pothole resilience, since shorter sidewalls absorb less. Winter setups often go the other way — a smaller wheel with a taller sidewall — for compliance and cheaper tires, again holding diameter steady.

A few practical notes complete the picture. Width changes affect grip, aquaplaning, and rim compatibility, not just looks, and each wheel width supports a limited tire-width range. Load index and speed rating must meet or exceed the vehicle's requirement regardless of geometry. And square comparisons matter: check the size actually on the car (door-jamb placard) rather than assuming the size on worn tires is original. Used with those habits, this calculator handles the geometry — the part that is pure math — and flags exactly when a professional's judgment on the rest is worth getting.

Quick Tips

  • 225/45R17 = 225 mm wide, sidewall 45% of width, 17-inch wheel.
  • Keep a new size within ~3% of the original overall diameter.
  • Larger tires make the speedometer under-read; smaller make it over-read.
  • Check the door-jamb placard for the factory size, not the worn tires.

Frequently Asked Questions

What do tire size numbers like 225/45R17 mean?

Width/aspect/wheel: 225 is the tread width in millimeters, 45 is the sidewall height as a percentage of that width (the aspect ratio), R means radial construction, and 17 is the wheel diameter in inches. Together they define the tire's overall size.

How is overall tire diameter calculated?

Wheel diameter plus two sidewalls: convert the wheel inches to millimeters (x 25.4), then add twice the sidewall height (width x aspect ratio). A 225/45R17 works out to about 634 mm, or roughly 25 inches overall.

Why does changing tire size affect my speedometer?

The speedometer counts wheel rotations and assumes the factory circumference. A larger tire covers more ground per rotation, so the speedometer under-reads; smaller over-reads. The calculator shows the exact percentage error between two sizes.

How much size difference is acceptable?

The common guideline is to keep the new overall diameter within about 3% of the original — beyond that, speedometer error grows, and gearing, clearance, and safety systems can be affected. For anything larger, or load and speed rating questions, a tire professional is the right check.
