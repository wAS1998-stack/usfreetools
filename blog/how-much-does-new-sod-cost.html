How Much Does New Sod Cost?

Sod typically runs $0.35–$0.60 per square foot for the material and $1.00–$2.00 per square foot installed. A 2,000 sq ft front lawn is therefore roughly $700–$1,200 in sod, or $2,000–$4,000 done for you.

Material, Pallet and Installed Pricing

Sod is sold three ways and the units confuse people. Farms quote per square foot ($0.35–$0.60 for common varieties), per roll (a standard US roll covers about 10 sq ft, so $3.50–$6.00), or per pallet (typically 450 sq ft, so $160–$270).

A 2,000 sq ft lawn with a 10% waste margin needs 2,200 sq ft — about 220 rolls or 5 pallets. Material cost: $770–$1,320. Delivery adds $50–$150 depending on distance.

Installed pricing of $1.00–$2.00 per square foot covers delivery, ground preparation, laying and initial rolling. That same lawn lands at $2,000–$4,000. The premium is heavy, time-sensitive labour, and it is honest work: sod must go down the day it arrives.

Variety matters too. Common Bermuda and fescue sit at the low end; zoysia, St. Augustine and premium bluegrass blends run 30–60% higher.

Sod vs Seed — the Real Comparison

Seed is dramatically cheaper. That same 2,000 sq ft lawn needs about 16 lbs of tall fescue for new seeding at $4–$8 a pound: $65–$130, roughly a tenth of sod's material cost. The grass seed calculator works out the rate for your species and area.

What the extra money buys with sod:

  • Instant lawn — usable in two to three weeks rather than a full season.
  • Weed suppression — a dense mat gives weeds no opening, where seeded lawns fight crabgrass through their first year.
  • Erosion control — on any slope, seed washes away and sod holds immediately.
  • A wider window — sod installs through most of the growing season; seed demands early fall for cool-season grasses or late spring for warm-season.

The practical compromise most landscapers use: sod the front yard where appearance matters now and the area is smaller, seed the back where cost scales painfully.

Working Out How Much You Need

Measure the actual lawn rather than the lot. Break odd shapes into rectangles and triangles, subtract beds, driveway and walkways, and add them up — lawns are consistently overestimated by eye, and sod is expensive enough that the error hurts.

Add 5–10% waste for the cuts along curves, beds and edges. The sod calculator converts your area into rolls and pallets with that margin included, and reports the approximate weight — which matters more than people expect.

Weight is a planning constraint. A single roll weighs 30–45 lbs depending on moisture; a pallet runs 1,500–3,000 lbs. Pallets arrive by truck with a forklift and land where the driver can reach. Plan drop points to shorten the wheelbarrow runs, and spread multiple pallets around a large yard rather than stacking them in one corner.

Ground Preparation Is Not Optional

Sod laid on unprepared ground fails, and it fails after you have paid for it. Proper preparation means: killing or removing existing grass and weeds, tilling or loosening the top 2–4 inches, grading so water runs away from the house, adding topsoil if the existing ground is poor, and raking to a fine, firm, level surface.

If the soil is thin or compacted — extremely common on new-build lots where topsoil was scraped — plan on 2–4 inches of imported topsoil first. The topsoil calculator prices that, and for a 2,000 sq ft lawn at 3 inches it is about 18 cubic yards, which is itself $450–$800. That line item surprises people and is the difference between a lawn that thrives and one that struggles for years.

Finished grade should sit about one inch below any hard edges, so the sod lies flush with walkways rather than proud of them.

Installation Day and the First Month

Sod is living turf harvested with an inch of soil attached, and stacked rolls begin composting within hours. Heat builds inside the pallet, and grass yellows in a day or two — faster in summer. The rule is simple: finish all preparation first, schedule delivery for the morning you will lay it, and install the same day.

Lay in staggered rows like brickwork, with tight seams and no overlaps, cutting pieces to fit with a knife. Water each section as it goes down rather than waiting until the end. Roll the finished lawn to press roots into contact with soil.

Then the first month decides the outcome: keep it consistently moist for the first two weeks, taper to deeper and less frequent watering as roots chase moisture down, stay off it, and hold the first mow until it has knitted down — tug a corner and resistance means rooting. Most sod failures are watering failures in week one, not installation failures.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a pallet of sod cost?

Typically $160-$270 for a pallet covering about 450 square feet, which works out to $0.35-$0.60 per square foot. Premium varieties like zoysia or St. Augustine run 30-60% higher than common fescue or Bermuda.

How much does it cost to sod a 2,000 square foot lawn?

About $770-$1,320 in sod alone (roughly 5 pallets with a 10% waste margin), or $2,000-$4,000 installed. If the site needs imported topsoil first, add roughly $450-800 for 18 cubic yards at 3 inches deep.

Is sod cheaper than seed?

No - seed costs roughly a tenth as much. The same 2,000 square foot lawn needs about 16 lbs of fescue seed at $65-130. Sod buys instant coverage, weed resistance, erosion control on slopes, and a much wider installation window.

How long does sod take to root?

Shallow rooting happens in about two weeks with consistent moisture, and full establishment takes roughly a month. Tug a corner: resistance means roots have taken hold and you can resume normal mowing and light traffic.
