How Much Does a Concrete Patio Cost?

A plain concrete patio typically runs $6–$12 per square foot installed, or roughly $2–$3 per square foot in materials if you pour it yourself. Here is where that range comes from, and how to work out your own number.

The Short Answer, With Honest Ranges

For a standard 4-inch slab with no decorative finish, most US homeowners pay somewhere between $6 and $12 per square foot installed. A 12×12 patio (144 sq ft) therefore lands around $865–$1,730, and a generous 16×20 (320 sq ft) around $1,920–$3,840. Those are contractor numbers: they include concrete, forms, reinforcement, labour, finishing and cleanup.

Doing it yourself changes the arithmetic completely. Concrete itself is cheap — the expense is the work. A 144 sq ft slab at 4 inches takes about 1.8 cubic yards, which is roughly 80 bags of 80 lb mix at $6–8 a bag ($480–$640) or a ready-mix delivery at $140–$180 a yard plus a short-load fee ($350–$550). Add gravel base, forms, rebar or mesh and you are realistically at $600–$900 in materials for that patio, plus a hard weekend.

Prices move with region, fuel costs and season, so treat every figure here as a planning range rather than a quote. The point is not the exact number — it is knowing whether a bid you receive is sane.

What Actually Drives the Price

Thickness. Four inches is standard for a patio. Six inches is for driveways and anything carrying vehicles, and it is 50% more concrete. Do not let a bid quietly drop you to three inches to look cheaper — thin slabs crack.

Site preparation. This is the hidden half of the job. A flat, accessible, already-cleared area is cheap. A sloped yard needing excavation and fill, a tight side gate that forces wheelbarrowing, or tree roots and old pavers to remove can add $1–$4 per square foot before a drop of concrete is poured.

Finish. A broom finish is included in base pricing. Stamped concrete typically adds $8–$18 per square foot; exposed aggregate, colouring or saw-cut patterns land in between. Decorative finishes can easily double the total, and they are where quotes diverge most.

Reinforcement and base. A compacted gravel base (4–6 inches) and wire mesh or rebar are not optional extras in most climates — they are what stops the slab moving. Expect them in any honest bid.

Access and volume. Ready-mix trucks charge short-load fees under about 3–4 yards, which is why small patios often cost more per yard than large ones. Two patios poured at once can be cheaper per square foot than one.

Estimating Your Own Slab in Two Minutes

Every estimate starts with volume, and volume is simple: length × width × thickness. The concrete calculator does it and converts to cubic yards and bag counts, then multiplies by whatever your local supplier charges.

Worked example — a 12×12 patio at 4 inches:

  • 12 × 12 × (4 ÷ 12) = 48 cubic feet
  • 48 ÷ 27 = 1.78 cubic yards
  • At 0.6 cu ft per 80 lb bag: 80 bags
  • Order 5–10% extra — forms bulge, subgrade is never perfectly flat

Then the base: the same patio needs about 1.8 cubic yards of gravel at 4 inches, which the gravel calculator prices the same way. Base material is the step most DIY budgets forget entirely.

Bags or Ready-Mix?

The crossover is around one cubic yard. Below that, bags win: no delivery minimum, no short-load fee, no clock ticking while you work. Above it, bags become punishing — a cubic yard is roughly 45 bags of 80 lb mix, each of which you lift, open, and mix.

There is also a quality argument. Ready-mix arrives at a consistent, specified strength. Hand-mixed bags vary with how much water each batch got, and a slab poured over three hours from inconsistent batches cures unevenly. For anything you will look at for twenty years, consistency matters.

The practical middle path many homeowners miss: a trailer-mounted mixer rental ($60–$100 a day) with bagged mix, which gets you consistency without a delivery minimum.

DIY vs Hiring Out — The Honest Comparison

Pouring concrete is physically demanding and unforgiving of hesitation. Once the truck tips or the first bags are mixed, you have a window measured in hours, and finishing — screeding, floating, edging, jointing, troweling — is genuine skill. A badly finished slab is visible forever and cannot be sanded out.

That said, a small, simple, ground-level patio is one of the more achievable DIY concrete jobs, and the savings are real: $600–$900 in materials against a $1,200+ installed bid on a 12×12. If you go that route, recruit help (three people minimum), pour early on a mild day, and have every tool laid out before the first bag opens.

Hire out when the slab is large, structural, sloped, decorative, or attached to the house. The premium buys speed, equipment and someone who has done it a thousand times.

Where Budgets Go Wrong

Forgetting the base. Gravel, compaction and sometimes drainage are a real line item, often 15–20% of the material cost.

Rounding down. Concrete is sold in whole yards and comes up short more often than long. Order 5–10% over and have a spot ready for the excess.

Ignoring control joints. Concrete will crack; joints decide where. Cutting them at roughly 2–3 times the slab thickness in feet (so every 8–10 feet for a 4-inch slab) is free and prevents random cracking.

Comparing bids that are not comparable. One quote with a 6-inch slab, rebar and a 6-inch base against one with 3.5 inches and no mesh is not a price difference — it is a different product. Ask every bidder for thickness, reinforcement, base depth and finish in writing.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a 12x12 concrete patio cost?

Installed, typically $865-$1,730 for a plain 4-inch broom-finished slab at $6-12 per square foot. Doing it yourself, materials run roughly $600-900 including gravel base - about 1.8 cubic yards of concrete, or 80 bags of 80 lb mix.

Is a concrete patio cheaper than pavers?

Usually yes for the base installation. Plain concrete runs about $6-12 per square foot installed while paver patios typically run $10-20. Pavers cost more up front but individual units can be lifted and reset, where a cracked slab is repaired or replaced wholesale.

How thick should a concrete patio be?

Four inches over a compacted gravel base is standard for foot traffic and patio furniture. Go to six inches if vehicles will ever drive on it. Thinner than four inches cracks under normal use regardless of what it saves.

Can I pour a concrete patio myself?

A small ground-level slab is achievable with help and preparation, and saves most of the labour cost. The difficulty is not mixing - it is finishing within the working window. Large, sloped, decorative or structural slabs are worth hiring out.
