How Much Does a Paver Patio Cost?

A paver patio typically runs $10–$20 per square foot installed, or about $4–$8 per square foot in materials if you build it yourself. The spread is almost entirely paver choice and how much digging the site needs.

The Numbers, Honestly

For standard concrete pavers over a proper base, most installed quotes land between $10 and $20 per square foot. A 12×12 patio (144 sq ft) is roughly $1,440–$2,880; a 16×20 (320 sq ft) roughly $3,200–$6,400. Natural stone, clay brick or large-format porcelain push well past that.

Building it yourself, the materials for a 144 sq ft patio look roughly like this: pavers at $2–$6 each (about 145 units for 12×12-inch pavers plus waste, so $290–$870), 1.8 cubic yards of base gravel ($55–$110), half a yard of bedding sand ($20–$40), polymeric joint sand ($30–$60), and edge restraint ($40–$80). Call it $450–$1,150 in materials, plus a plate compactor rental at $70–$100 a day.

The labour gap is enormous here — far larger than with concrete — because a paver patio is mostly excavation and base work, and that is pure physical time rather than skill.

Why the Base Costs More Than People Expect

A paver patio is not pavers on dirt. The standard section is 4–6 inches of compacted crushed gravel, 1 inch of bedding sand, then the pavers, held in by edge restraint and locked with joint sand. For a driveway, the base goes to 8–12 inches.

That base is where the money and the time go, and it is where cheap installations cut corners. Six inches of gravel under a 320 sq ft patio is about 6 cubic yards of material — several tons to deliver, move and compact in 2-inch lifts. A quote that seems remarkably low is usually one that plans to lay pavers on 2 inches of sand and nothing else. That patio will be wavy within two seasons.

The paver calculator sizes all three layers at once — paver count, base gravel and bedding sand — so you can price the whole section rather than just the visible part.

Paver Choice Is the Biggest Lever

Standard concrete pavers ($2–$4 each) are the value option and come in enough shapes and colours for most projects.

Premium concrete ($4–$7) buys tumbled edges, better colour blends and larger formats that lay faster.

Clay brick ($5–$9) holds colour permanently because the colour is the fired clay, not a surface pigment — the traditional choice that ages best.

Natural stone (bluestone, travertine, flagstone, $10–$30+) is where budgets break. Irregular thickness also slows installation considerably, which raises labour on top of material.

A useful exercise: price your patio at two paver tiers. The difference on 320 sq ft between $2.50 and $6 pavers is roughly $1,300 — often more than the entire base package.

Working Out Your Own Quantities

Paver count is area divided by one paver's area, plus waste. A 12×12-inch paver covers exactly one square foot, so 144 sq ft needs 144 before waste. Smaller units need more arithmetic: a 4×8-inch brick covers 32 square inches, so 4.5 of them per square foot — 648 bricks for the same patio.

Waste margins matter more outdoors than indoors because every edge is a cut and offcuts rarely reuse. Use 10% for a plain rectangle and 15% or more for curves, circles or a diagonal pattern. Buy it all in one delivery: pavers vary subtly between production runs, and a patch from next season's batch shows.

Base and sand are volume calculations: area × depth ÷ 27 for cubic yards. The gravel calculator handles the base, and remember that gravel is usually sold by the ton, not the yard — roughly 1.4 tons per cubic yard for crushed stone.

DIY Is Genuinely Viable Here

Unlike concrete, a paver patio has no working-time pressure. Nothing cures, nothing sets, and you can stop at the end of any day and resume next weekend. Mistakes are reversible — lift the paver, fix the bedding, put it back. That makes it one of the best value DIY projects in home improvement: you are replacing $1,500–$4,000 of labour with weekends and a rented compactor.

The honest cost is physical. A 320 sq ft patio at a 6-inch base means moving roughly 6 cubic yards of gravel in and a similar volume of soil out — about 15 tons of material through a wheelbarrow. Budget two or three weekends and expect the excavation to take longer than the laying.

Rent the plate compactor rather than hand-tamping. Compaction in 2-inch lifts is the single thing that determines whether the patio is still flat in five years, and a hand tamper cannot do it properly over any real area.

Costs People Forget

Disposal. Excavated soil has to go somewhere. A dumpster or haul-away is $150–$500 unless you have space on site.

Edge restraint. Cheap, essential, and skipped constantly. Without it the field creeps outward and joints open.

Polymeric sand. More expensive than plain sand and worth it — it hardens to resist weeds and washout.

Cutting. A masonry blade and an angle grinder work for a few cuts; past that, rent a wet saw ($50–$90 a day) and save hours.

Slope. Plan about ¼ inch of fall per foot away from the house. It costs nothing at layout time and is expensive to retrofit.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a 12x12 paver patio cost?

Installed, typically $1,440-$2,880 at $10-20 per square foot. Doing it yourself, materials run roughly $450-$1,150 - about 145 pavers plus 1.8 cubic yards of base gravel, bedding sand, joint sand and edge restraint.

Are pavers cheaper than concrete?

No, pavers typically cost more to install - about $10-20 per square foot against $6-12 for plain concrete. The trade-off is repairability: a damaged paver lifts out and is replaced in minutes, while a cracked slab is patched visibly or replaced entirely.

How deep should the base be under pavers?

Four to six inches of compacted crushed gravel for a patio or walkway, topped with exactly one inch of bedding sand. Driveways need 8-12 inches. Compacting in 2-inch lifts matters more than total depth - uncompacted base settles unevenly no matter how thick it is.

Can I lay pavers directly on sand?

It works for a season and then fails. Without a compacted gravel base the pavers settle unevenly, rock underfoot, and heave with freeze-thaw. The base is the project; the pavers are the finish.
