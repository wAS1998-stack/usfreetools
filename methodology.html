Methodology

How every calculator and conversion on this site is worked out, and where the constants come from.

Last updated: 2026-10-31

Where our formulas come from

Every calculator on this site implements a published formula or a documented industry standard. We do not invent coefficients, and we do not adjust results to make them look tidier. Where a figure is a convention rather than a law of nature — waste margins, seeding rates, typical prices — we say so on the page and give the range rather than a single confident number.

The primary sources behind the calculators that make factual claims:

  • Wind chill and heat index — the National Weather Service formulas (the 2001 wind chill revision and the Rothfusz heat-index regression), applied only within the temperature and wind ranges the NWS defines them for.
  • Unit conversions — exact definitions published by NIST: the inch is exactly 2.54 cm, the pound exactly 0.45359237 kg. These are definitions, not measurements, so our conversions are exact to the precision shown.
  • Stairs, roof pitch and drywall — geometry limits from the International Residential Code. Local jurisdictions amend the IRC, so we state the common limits and tell you to confirm with your building department.
  • Pay and overtime — the federal Fair Labor Standards Act rules published by the US Department of Labor. State rules can be stricter.
  • Mortgage and down payment — standard amortisation mathematics, with PMI thresholds as described by the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau.
  • Health calculators — BMI as defined by the CDC; ideal-weight figures from the Devine and Robinson formulas used in clinical dosing; calorie estimates from the Mifflin-St Jeor equation. These are population-level estimates, not medical advice.
  • Lawn and soil — seeding rates and establishment guidance from university cooperative extension services.
  • Dog age — the 2020 epigenetic-clock research (Wang et al., Cell Systems), not the multiply-by-seven myth.
  • Sales tax rates by state — state and average local rates from the Tax Foundation’s mid-2026 survey (rates as of July 1, 2026). Local rates change often, so the calculator lets you type the exact rate for your address.
  • Firewood — cord definitions from university extension services; weight and heat per cord by species from Utah State University Forestry Extension; the 80 cubic feet of solid wood in a stacked cord from Purdue Extension.
  • Pay raises — the typical-raise context comes from The Conference Board’s survey of US salary-increase budgets.
  • GPA conversions — CBSE’s CGPA × 9.5 formula for the 10-point scale, and the College Board’s guidance that letter-grade cut-offs vary by school.

How we handle prices

Several calculators now estimate cost as well as quantity. Those prices are editable inputs seeded with typical US ranges, not quotes. We seed them so the tool is useful before you have called a supplier, and we label them as approximate because material pricing varies by region, season and volume. The number that matters is the one you get after typing in your own supplier's price.

Our cost guides quote installed-price ranges gathered from contractor and supplier pricing. They are planning figures. Anyone who tells you a patio costs exactly one number without seeing your site is guessing.

What we deliberately do not do

We do not generate thousands of near-identical pages to chase search traffic. In October 2026 we removed roughly seven thousand such pages from our index — templated variations that differed only in their numbers — because they made the site worse for the people using it. What remains is the tools themselves and writing that took real time.

We do not publish invented statistics, fake user counts, or review scores nobody gave us. We do not run calculators that send your data anywhere: every tool on this site computes in your browser, and nothing you type is transmitted to us.

Accuracy and corrections

Formulas and constants on this site were last reviewed on 31 October 2026. Calculators that cite a standard carry that source and review date on the page itself.

If you find a wrong answer, a stale constant, or a code limit that has changed, tell us and we will fix it and note the correction. Reference sites earn trust by being correctable, not by claiming to be perfect.
